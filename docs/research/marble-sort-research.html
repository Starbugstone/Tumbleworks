Research & proposed game brief · 30 October 2026

One marble game, three kinds of levels

One continuous campaign that alternates between sorting tubes, managing a conveyor, and collecting triples from a layered tray.

Confirmed direction: all three styles are different levels within the same game. Use one level map, one progress record, and a consistent visual identity. Each level presents one complete ruleset. Introduce two boards of each type, then alternate them. A proposed first release contains thirty levels: ten of each type. Later expansion can bring the total to ninety, with thirty per type.

Scope and confidence. This is desk research across publisher pages, official store descriptions and screenshots, developer documentation, and specialist references. It is not a hands-on mobile playthrough or reverse engineering. Specific advertised games need not share every rule. Rules, counts, level ideas, targets, and estimates labeled “proposed” below are our design choices, not claims about the mobile games.

What game are we adapting?

The publisher’s Marble Sort! page describes top-box releases, a moving circular conveyor, matching trays, one-tap interaction, and a belt that can clog. [1] The official Google Play rules specify three same-color marbles per receiving box and failure when the belt is full with no matching box available. [2]

[4] Triple Match 3D’s official support describes a seven-slot collection bar and clearing three identical items; Peak’s Match Factory support similarly describes matching triples, level goals, and losses from a full bar or expired timer. [26] [28] Their pieces are often varied objects. A color-and-pattern marble tray is our adaptation of that matching structure, not an exact recreation.

Campaign structure: levels 1–2 teach tubes, 3–4 teach tray matching, and 5–6 teach the conveyor. From level 7, rotate tube → tray → conveyor. A later level reuses a familiar ruleset with a new puzzle. Keep the next level's type visible on the map, and show a brief rules reminder on entry. The player progresses through one campaign rather than choosing separate modes.

Level type: tube sorting

Proposed base: each tube holds four marbles. For C colors, use four marbles of each color and C + 2 tubes, initially leaving two tubes empty. Select a source, then a destination. Move one top marble only when the destination is empty or its top marble matches, and there is room. Win when each color occupies one full tube and the remaining tubes are empty. Use no timer.

The player is managing working space. An empty tube is a resource that lets them expose buried colors. Splitting and recombining temporary groups creates the puzzle. Show a selected marble clearly, explain invalid moves, and support both two taps and keyboard selection. Avoid requiring precision dragging.

Concrete level idea: two tubes contain interleaved red and blue while green is split across the other tubes. The useful move is to consolidate green into a spare tube, freeing access to the alternating colors. This is a design recipe; no particular board layout or solution is claimed here.

Generation: balance counts, produce a candidate, and solve-check it. Starting with a solved board and applying ordinary legal moves cannot create mixed-color stacks: such moves only split or combine existing monochrome groups. A reverse generator therefore needs specially constructed predecessor states whose forward inverse moves are legal. Randomly mixing colors is not a solvability guarantee either.

Keep tube state as ordered color arrays plus capacity. The legal-move predicate must be identical in play and the solver. Preserve an actual witness, respect a search budget, and treat an unresolved search as unverified. The tube-sorting research and solver references already collected are directly relevant to this mode. [18] [19]

Level type: tray matching

Reference behavior: Triple Match 3D describes hidden objects in a pile and matching triplets. Its support page specifies seven collection slots and removing three identical items. [26] [27] Match Factory provides a related goal-based, timed interpretation; its publisher describes a physical 3D presentation. [28] [29]

Proposed marble adaptation: put patterned colored marbles into fixed shallow pockets arranged in layers. A marble is selectable when none of its defined blockers remain. Tapping moves it to a seven-slot rack. Immediately remove any three marbles of the same type, then check fullness. Clear every marble to win. If seven unmatched marbles remain after resolution, the level is lost. An available third match selected into the seventh slot must clear successfully rather than trigger a premature loss.

Use no timer in the main campaign. Add a separate timed challenge later if wanted. This keeps the main mode about choosing what to uncover, rather than fast visual scanning. Show which marbles are selectable and why partly visible ones are blocked. A fixed pocket layout and explicit blocker graph let us verify levels and restore undo exactly; uncontrolled physical piles are a separate, larger implementation choice.

Important capacity deduction: with immediate triple clearing, each type can occupy at most two rack slots. With only three types, the rack holds at most six unmatched pieces; the next pick completes a triple. Therefore a seven-slot rack only develops genuine overflow pressure with at least four types. Two- and three-type boards are suitable safe tutorials, not capacity challenges. This follows from our rules, not an external difficulty measurement.

These counts are proposed content ranges, not verified layouts. At least one complete triple of each type is required, so scale counts consistently and keep every type's total divisible by three when the goal is full clearance. Prefer reusing patterns and colors over dozens of visually similar marble textures.

Concrete level idea: the rack already has two red and two blue pieces. A third red is beneath a green triple that can be cleared immediately; the third blue is below several different colors. Choosing the green route frees the red set with less rack pressure. The authored layout must establish that dependency and be verified before use.

Generation: begin with an acyclic cover graph and a known selectable removal order. Assign types and interleave matches while keeping that witness below the rack limit. Verify the result using the exact blocker and match-resolution rules. Equal color counts alone do not prove that the buried third pieces are reachable before overflow.

Solver state: remaining marble IDs, rack contents or per-type residual counts, and derived available pieces. Hit testing and blocker rules must agree across touch, mouse, and rendering. Test fast repeated taps, picks during match animations, two identical pieces arriving together, and selection of the final piece.

One campaign, three rule engines

Share: marble materials and symbols, board framing, lighting, sounds, responsive input, focus handling, undo infrastructure, settings, progress UI, saving, telemetry, hosting, and optional portal integration.

Keep type-specific: legal actions, state, completion and dead-end detection, level schemas, generators, solvers, hint explanations, and camera framing. A matching rack, a tube, and a conveyor slot have different semantics. Reuse the shell while giving each set of rules a clear owner. These are internal rule engines, not separate player-facing modes.

A practical interface is validateLevel → createState → availableActions → applyAction → getStatus → serialize/restore, with optional simulation-time advancement for the conveyor. Use a tagged level-type field in definitions and saved snapshots so a tube board cannot be interpreted as tray data. Implement hints using each type's own state and search rules. The shared undo manager stores complete board snapshots.

Shared progression: persist one current global level, completed level IDs, best records, settings, and the current board's typed snapshot. Reset board resources when moving to the next level; leftover conveyor marbles never become tube contents. Undo applies to the current board. Use one completion marker and shared cosmetic milestones; optional skill medals can have different criteria for each type.

Difficulty: global level number is not the same as experience with a particular ruleset. Tune each type by its occurrence count, so global level 8, the third tray puzzle, teaches a tray concept rather than assuming eight tray levels of experience. Each new rule should be introduced in an easy board before combining it with other constraints.

Proposed delivery: build the shared shell and tube rules first because they exercise deterministic actions with little animation complexity; add the conveyor's timed deliveries; then add the tray's picking and blocker graph. Make five boards for each, giving a 15-level test campaign, before investing in a large campaign. Target ten polished boards of each type for a 30-board public release, followed by thirty each for 90 total. The first six global levels keep paired introductions; later levels alternate types. These are content targets, not a requirement to ship 90 boards immediately.

Scope correction: the detailed conveyor plan and effort table below describe that workstream in isolation. They are not a quote for all three level types. Sharing assets saves work, but authoring, validation, testing, and tutorial design are still needed three times. Re-estimate the combined project after the three small prototypes.

Level type: conveyor sorting — detailed workstream

The enjoyable loop is anticipation → release → motion → delivery → a newly exposed choice. The next decision should become readable as the current action resolves. The pleasure comes from seeing a carefully chosen pour clear several requests in succession.

The strategic resource is temporary storage. A marble with no receiver is not automatically a mistake: it may need to wait while the player exposes the right box. The puzzle comes from balancing supply access, receiver order, and the space needed for the next useful pour.

The App Store update history shows how the mobile version expands this foundation: larger pours, elevators, turn-dependent gates, and color-specific barriers. We can borrow the general idea of gradually adding dependencies while designing our own behavior and levels. [3]

Individual store reviews describe both the appeal of planning and relaxation and frustration with expensive tools or difficult progression. These are examples of player concerns, not a representative survey. Our opportunity is an understandable puzzle with easy recovery and a clear reason for failure. [2]

Proposed first-version rules

  1. Supplies: three or four lanes of packets. Only the exposed packet nearest the belt can be released; removing it exposes the next. Each packet shows its color, symbol, and marble count.
  2. Pours: begin with three marbles, then introduce six and nine. A tap releases one complete packet. This is a proposed simplification, not a claim about every mobile supply.
  3. Belt: one directed loop with a proposed normal capacity of 24 marble slots. Incoming marbles reserve slots immediately, so repeated taps cannot overbook the belt.
  4. Receivers: usually four lanes. Each exposed box wants three matching marbles; completing it reveals the next box. Show at least the next two requests so planning is informed.
  5. Delivery: automatic at fixed pickup points in the belt’s direction of travel. Specify stable priority if multiple boxes request the same color. Unmatched marbles continue circulating.
  6. Admission: a packet is available only if its full count fits into currently unreserved space. Show a clear “needs 6 spaces” explanation when it does not.
  7. Win: every required box is complete, all supplies are released, and no marble remains in transit or on the belt.
  8. Jam: no packet can be admitted and no current or scheduled delivery can free space. A temporarily full belt that is about to drain is not a loss. Any other solver-proven dead end may offer a warning; do not declare losses using a guess.
  9. Recovery: free restart, pause, unlimited undo to the snapshot before a release, and an explainable hint. Undo restores the belt and receiver progress as well as supplies.

Pacing: keep the belt visibly moving, allow the player to think indefinitely, and provide a hold-to-fast-forward or “finish current deliveries” control. Aim initially for 30–90 seconds in early levels and 1–3 minutes later; these are playtest targets. Start directly with an interactive tutorial.

Rewards: a clear completion animation and chapter progress are enough. If we add medals, reward low peak congestion or solving without hints. Since every packet must usually be released, counting taps alone is a weak measure of skill.

Additional level recipes: a color buried behind a temporarily unwanted packet; two receivers competing for one color; a large pour that must serve several consecutive boxes; a marked delivery that unlocks a lane; a large order that delays the next request; a supply floor that cannot advance until every front packet is cleared. Later experiments could include a visible turn gate, a deterministic diverter, or a two-color packet. Introduce each separately and rerun solvability checks.

Keep information readable. Prefer preview windows and visible dependencies to hidden random colors. If a mystery packet is introduced later, either ensure success is possible without knowing its color or present the mode honestly as a risk puzzle. Do not mix forced guessing into the main logic campaign.

A concrete puzzle, with checked release orders

This original example uses only two receiver lanes to make the dependency easy to see. Queues below are front first. “Blue ×6 → Red ×6” means blue must be released before red in supply A.

BYellow ×6 → Green ×6RightGreen → Green → Yellow → Red → Green
CRed ×3 → Green ×3

With 15 belt spaces: A → A → B → B → C → C solves it if we let deliveries settle after each pour. The first blue packet waits; the following red packet clears the two red boxes and exposes the blue receivers. Releasing A → B → C → C instead leaves 12 unmatched marbles, only 3 free slots, and only 6-marble packets available. Neither exposed receiver can clear those stored colors: the machine is jammed.

With 24 belt spaces: that same opening can recover; A → B → C → C → A → B solves the board. This is a concrete example of changing difficulty through working space rather than simply adding colors.

Verification limit: I checked color conservation, all three example winning traces, and the tighter board’s jam trace in a small deterministic planning simulation. It lets all possible deliveries settle between actions and does not model continuous travel times, gate timing, or overlapping pours. The shipped runtime must replay these witnesses and validate its own timing rules. Data: level-examples.json; results: verification record.

How to produce levels that are actually solvable

Begin with authored puzzles. The first 10–12 should each teach a specific idea. Use a small internal editor to arrange supply queues, receiver queues, capacity, previews, locks, and goals. It should show per-color supply/demand totals, replay a solution, and flag unsupported mechanics.

Then generate around a known plan. Construct a valid release-and-delivery schedule, distribute packets into lanes while preserving that schedule, and verify it with the actual rules. Vary one parameter at a time. An equal number of red marbles and red slots is necessary but does not establish that their order can work.

Use a planning solver offline. Search deterministic states with memoization and bounded work. Include supply fronts, receiver progress, stored marble order, locks, and any turn state. If timing matters, also include belt phase and in-flight events, or search a conservative settled abstraction and verify its witness in the real runtime. A timeout means “not verified,” not “impossible.”

Store a witness, not just a seed. Ship stable authored level definitions with a rules version, level version, generator seed if relevant, and at least one proven solution. Build hints from a solution to the player’s current state; replaying the initial solution blindly may be wrong after a different opening.

Measure human difficulty separately. Useful signals are attempts to completion, time spent deciding versus watching, undo use, maximum congestion, dead-end rate, and abandonment. Also inspect how many early choices stay recoverable and how far ahead the player must reason. Larger boards and longer solvers do not automatically mean better puzzles. King’s GDC session overview likewise treats tools, testing, and data-informed iteration as part of level production. [17]

Keep mode claims separate: the tube research and Kociemba solver apply to tube/bin sorting. Neither establishes the complexity or behavior of the conveyor or tray rules. Each needs its own validator and solution witnesses.

Browser details that need deliberate handling

  • Phone and desktop layout: mouse and touch use the same single-click/tap action. Maintain large hit areas, a readable preview, and safe space around browser controls. Reflow the machine for wide screens instead of stretching a narrow portrait board.
  • Audio startup: initialize or resume audio on the first player gesture; browsers may block unsolicited playback. [12]
  • Tab changes: pause simulation and audio when hidden; resume without a huge elapsed-time catch-up. Browsers throttle timers and usually stop animation callbacks in background tabs. [13]
  • Saving: write versioned saves at stable actions, guard storage access, validate loaded data, and retain a fallback when saving is unavailable. Local storage is tied to the origin; private-session progress is not permanent and local saving does not provide cross-device sync. [14]
  • Offline: add service-worker caching if requested. Offline play starts after assets have been successfully cached; merely hosting a static game does not provide it. [15]
  • Color accessibility: use stable symbols or patterns on packets and receivers, and an optional high-contrast marble pattern. Avoid making red/green discrimination essential. [16]
  • Motion and keyboard: provide reduced-motion settings, clear focus indicators, and keyboard access to lanes and recovery controls. A full screen-reader gameplay mode would need separate design and testing; visible symbols alone do not provide it.

Proposed performance budgets: aim for an initial compressed download under 5 MB, 60 fps on the chosen reference devices, and a usable reduced-quality mode on slower hardware. These are engineering targets, not measurements. Profile a busy board with every allowed in-flight and belt marble; keep simultaneous object counts bounded, reuse geometry, and limit device-pixel ratio and expensive reflections.

Required proof before release: replay every level witness; verify conservation and capacity limits; confirm undo and save/reload at each meaningful transition; test rapid repeated taps, temporary fullness, last-box completion, resize, hidden-tab resume, blocked storage, and failed optional platform calls. Check real iOS Safari and Android Chrome as well as desktop Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge. Compare acceptance of the same actions across frame rates.

Custom art, physical simulation, a broad solver, and device issues can increase effort substantially. A money budget needs the developer’s rate and an agreed art scope.

Asset needs: one sphere mesh, a few modular tray/box pieces, the belt and machine frame, color/symbol materials, a readable UI font, a small control-icon set, roughly 6–10 short sound effects, and a few restrained completion effects. Build the board from primitives first. Establish our own name, visual identity, sounds, implementation, and level layouts.

Defer large systems: accounts, competitive leaderboards, cloud sync, shops, multiple currencies, energy/lives, seasonal passes, and hundreds of levels are not prerequisites for validating this game. Daily puzzles can initially be selected from a verified pool with a stable date-to-level mapping; competitive fairness and trusted scores would require more server support.

Own website: a static release is the simplest initial distribution route. It gives us control over presentation and monetization but does not supply an audience. A small no-ad test is the cleanest way to observe the game loop.

Poki: optional later distribution, subject to curation. Its current requirements include desktop/mobile/tablet support, a 16:9-scalable canvas, bundled resources, resilient storage handling, and platform-controlled ads. Its HTML5 SDK needs correct loading and gameplay lifecycle events. Build a portal layout and adapter rather than assuming a portrait standalone page is immediately submission-ready. [21] [22]

CrazyGames: currently distinguishes Basic Launch, without monetization, from a selected Full Launch requiring fuller integration. Its technical requirements specify an initial download no larger than 50 MB, with 20 MB for mobile-homepage eligibility. Our proposed 5 MB budget is deliberately tighter. Acceptance and revenue are not guaranteed. [23] [24]

Monetization, if wanted: choose after the prototype proves engaging. An optional extra hint or cosmetic theme is a better first experiment than designing unavoidable failures. Any portal release must follow that platform’s ad rules and pause correctly around ad breaks.

What to learn from the first playtest

Ask a few people unfamiliar with the design to play without coaching. Watch whether they understand the first move, notice receiver previews, know why a pour is unavailable, and understand the first jam. Track level starts/completions, retries, undo/hint use, peak belt occupancy, errors, and time spent waiting versus deciding. Small samples identify obvious problems; they do not establish reliable retention or revenue forecasts.

Proceed to more content when players can explain the rules and want another board. Simplify if they tap randomly, miss tiny previews, or cannot understand losses. Speed up or skip delivery animations if waiting dominates. Increase dependency depth only after the preceding idea is understood.

The updated first implementation brief: one mixed campaign with three level types, deterministic rules, original toy-like 3D styling, undo/restart/hints, shared saved progress, and five authored test boards for each type. Polish ten of each type for the first public release; expand only after the core interactions and tutorials work for new players.
